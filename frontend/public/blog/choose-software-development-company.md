# How to Choose the Right Software Development Company for Your Business

You need software built. You have a budget. And now you are looking at a dozen development companies, each claiming to be the best fit.

How do you decide?

Most business owners approach this like hiring a contractor for home renovation — they compare prices and portfolios. But software development is fundamentally different. A beautiful portfolio does not guarantee clean code. A low price does not mean good value. And a smooth sales call does not mean reliable delivery.

This guide explains how to evaluate a software development company using the same criteria we have refined over years of building and auditing projects for businesses across Nepal. It includes a practical checklist you can print out and use during every vendor meeting.

---

## The 10 Evaluation Criteria That Actually Matter

| Criterion | What to look for | Red flags |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Technical expertise | Proven experience with your technology stack | Generic "we know everything" responses |
| 2. Portfolio quality | Projects similar to yours in scope and industry | Only screenshots, no live demos |
| 3. Communication | Clear, regular updates in your preferred channel | Slow responses, language barriers |
| 4. Project management | Defined methodology and timeline | Vague estimates, no milestones |
| 5. Security practices | Documented security processes and compliance | No mention of security |
| 6. Documentation | Technical and user documentation included | "Documentation is extra" |
| 7. Pricing transparency | Clear, itemized pricing | Hidden fees, "final price TBD" |
| 8. Source code ownership | Clear statement that you own the code | Unclear ownership terms |
| 9. Deployment support | Help with going live and post-launch | "We just build, you deploy" |
| 10. Post-launch support | Defined maintenance and support terms | No support after launch |

Let us look at each in detail.

## 1. Technical Expertise: Do They Actually Know What You Need?

### What to look for

Ask them to explain their approach to a specific technical challenge in your project. A good company can:

- Explain the trade-offs between different technologies
- Recommend an architecture that fits your scale and budget
- Identify potential technical risks early

For example, if you need a real-time chat feature, ask whether they would use WebSockets, Server-Sent Events, or a third-party service like Twilio. Their answer tells you whether they understand the underlying technology or are just parroting marketing talk.

### Questions to ask

- "What technology stack do you recommend for my project and why?"
- "How would you handle [specific technical challenge in your project]?"
- "Can you walk me through a project where you faced a similar challenge?"

### Red flags

- They say "we use the latest framework" without explaining why it suits your needs
- They cannot explain their technical choices in plain language
- They claim to have experience in every possible technology
- When you ask about specific tools, they deflect or change the subject

## 2. Portfolio Quality: Look Beyond the Screenshots

### What to look for

Don't just look at the portfolio website. Ask to see:

- **Live projects** — visit the actual websites or apps
- **Case studies** — how they approached problems, what they built, measurable results
- **Projects in your industry** — not required, but helpful for understanding domain knowledge
- **Before and after** — if applicable, see what they transformed

### Questions to ask

- "Can I see three projects similar in scope to mine?"
- "What was your role in each project shown in the portfolio?"
- "Can you show me the admin panel or back-end as well as the front-end?"
- "Do you have references from clients in my industry?"

### Red flags

- Only stock photos and mockups in the portfolio
- No live links or case studies
- Every project looks identical (they use templates)
- They cannot explain their specific contribution to the projects shown

> **Our experience:** At [hedztech.com](https://hedztech.com), we always provide live demos, GitHub access to code repositories (where appropriate), and references from at least two recent clients. If a company won't do this, ask why.

## 3. Communication: Can You Actually Understand Each Other?

### What to look for

Communication is the #1 predictor of project success. You want a company where:

- Responses come within 24 hours (or whatever SLA they promise)
- Technical concepts are explained in business terms
- You are speaking directly with the people who will build your project
- There is a single point of contact who understands your project

### Questions to ask

- "Who will be my main point of contact?"
- "What communication channels do you use (email, Slack, phone, video calls)?"
- "How often will I get updates and in what format?"
- "What time zone are your developers in and what are their working hours?"

### Red flags

- The salesperson who sold you is different from the project manager who will run it
- Vague promises about communication ("we'll keep you updated")
- Language barriers that make it hard to explain requirements
- Only one person in the company handles client communication, creating a bottleneck

---

## 4. Project Management: How Will They Deliver?

### What to look for

A professional development company uses a structured methodology. Ask them:

- **What methodology** (Agile, Scrum, Kanban, Waterfall)
- **What tools** they use for project tracking (Jira, Trello, Asana, Monday.com)
- **How they handle changes** to scope
- **How they report progress** (weekly calls, monthly reports, shared dashboards)

### Questions to ask

- "Walk me through your development process from start to finish."
- "How do you handle scope changes or new feature requests during development?"
- "What happens if a milestone is missed?"
- "How do I track progress? Can I see the work in progress?"

### Red flags

- They say "we just start coding and finish when done"
- No defined milestones or deliverables
- No project management tool or process
- They promise delivery dates without discussing technical dependencies

---

## 5. Security Practices: Is Your Data at Risk?

### What to look for

Security is not optional. Ask about:

- **Data handling** — how they protect your data during development
- **Authentication** — secure login systems (OAuth, 2FA)
- **Data encryption** — at rest and in transit
- **Compliance** — GDPR, PCI-DSS, local data protection laws if applicable
- **Code review** — do they review each other's code?
- **Testing** — automated tests, security scanning

### Questions to ask

- "How do you protect sensitive data during development?"
- "What security measures do you implement in your code?"
- "Do you perform security testing?"
- "What happens if there is a security issue after launch?"

### Red flags

- No mention of security in their process
- They store passwords in plain text
- They use outdated libraries or frameworks
- They cannot explain their security practices

---

## 6. Documentation: Will You Be Able to Maintain It Later?

### What to look for

Documentation is often the first thing cut when budgets run over. Insist on:

- **Technical documentation** — API docs, architecture diagrams, deployment instructions
- **User documentation** — admin guides, user manuals, training materials
- **Code comments** — well-commented code that explains business logic
- **Runbooks** — step-by-step recovery procedures for critical systems

### Questions to ask

- "What documentation do you provide?"
- "Is documentation included in the estimate or billed separately?"
- "How do you handle onboarding for new developers who need to maintain the code?"

### Red flags

- "Documentation is extra cost"
- They cannot produce any documentation from past projects
- Code is poorly commented or uses unclear naming conventions

---

## 7. Pricing Transparency: What Does It Really Cost?

### What to look for

Transparent companies provide:

- An **itemized breakdown** of costs
- A clear definition of what is **in scope** and **out of scope**
- A **payment schedule** tied to milestones
- A process for handling **cost overruns** and **extra work**

### Questions to ask

- "Can you break down the cost by phase or feature?"
- "What is included in the estimate and what is extra?"
- "How do you handle requests for changes during development?"
- "What is your payment schedule?"

### Red flags

- A single flat price with no breakdown
- Vague terms like "estimated cost" or "ballpark figure"
- Payment upfront in full (more than 30%)
- No process for handling scope changes

---

## 8. Source Code Ownership: Do You Actually Own What You Pay For?

### What to look for

The source code is your intellectual property. You should:

- **Own the code** outright once payment is complete
- **Have access** to the code repository during development
- **Be able to switch** developers without losing your code

### Questions to ask

- "Who owns the source code after development?"
- "Can I access the code repository during development?"
- "What happens if we decide to switch developers midway?"
- "Do you sign NDA and IP assignment agreements?"

### Red flags

- Unclear ownership terms
- They refuse to share code during development
- They claim ownership of "their" code even though you paid for it
- No IP assignment agreement

---

## 9. Deployment Support: They Build It, You Deploy It

### What to look for

Deployment is where many projects fail. A good company:

- Has experience with your deployment environment
- Provides step-by-step deployment instructions
- Can handle the deployment themselves if you prefer
- Tests the deployment before going live

### Questions to ask

- "Will you handle the deployment, or do I need to find someone else?"
- "What is your process for deployment and rollback?"
- "Do you provide staging and production environments?"
- "What happens if something breaks after launch?"

### Red flags

- "We just deliver the code and you figure out deployment"
- No experience with your hosting environment
- No staging environment for testing
- No rollback plan

---

## 10. Post-Launch Support: The Project Doesn't End at Launch

### What to look for

Software needs ongoing maintenance. A professional company:

- Offers a support period after launch (typically 30–90 days)
- Provides a maintenance plan with clear SLAs
- Has a process for bug fixes vs. new features
- Gives you a way to request support

### Questions to ask

- "What support is included after launch?"
- "How do you handle bug reports?"
- "What is your SLA for bug fixes?"
- "Do you offer ongoing maintenance plans?"

### Red flags

- "No support included — that's extra"
- No defined SLA for bug fixes
- They disappear after launch
- They charge for every minor fix

## Red Flags That Should Never Be Ignored

### 1. The "Yes" Company

They agree to everything you ask. No questions. No pushback. No realistic timeline. They want to be everyone's vendor and will disappoint everyone.

**Why it matters:** A good development partner tells you when your ideas are technically challenging, expensive, or unnecessary. Silence is not a virtue in software development.

### 2. No In-House Developers

The company outsources all development to freelancers they cannot control. You never know who is actually writing your code.

**Why it matters:** Quality standards, communication, and accountability become impossible to enforce.

### 3. No Contract

They want to start immediately, no paperwork. "We'll figure it out as we go."

**Why it matters:** Without a written agreement, you have no recourse if things go wrong.

### 4. No Questions About Your Business

They jump straight to technical solutions without understanding your goals, audience, or constraints.

**Why it matters:** Software built without understanding the business problem is just expensive art.

### 5. Unrealistic Timelines

A 6-month project estimated at 3 weeks. A complex ecommerce site priced at $500.

**Why it matters:** If the timeline or price doesn't match reality, the quality will not match expectations.

## The Interview Process: What to Ask in Your First Meeting

Here is a practical script for your first meeting with any development company:

### Round 1: Discovery (30–45 minutes)

1. "Tell me about your experience with projects like mine."
2. "What technology stack do you recommend and why?"
3. "What are the biggest technical risks in this project?"
4. "Who will be working on my project and what are their qualifications?"
5. "Do you have a portfolio piece that is similar to what I need?"

### Round 2: Process (30 minutes)

1. "Walk me through your development process."
2. "How do you handle changes in scope?"
3. "What tools do you use for project tracking and communication?"
4. "How often will I get updates and in what format?"
5. "What happens if a milestone is missed?"

### Round 3: Contract and Ownership (15 minutes)

1. "What are the payment terms?"
2. "Who owns the source code?"
3. "What support is included after launch?"
4. "What is your process for bug fixes vs. new features?"
5. "Can I get a reference from a recent client?"

## Working with Local vs International Companies

### Local (Nepal-based) Advantages

- Same time zone and language
- Easier to meet in person for initial discussions
- Lower cost of living translates to competitive rates
- Better understanding of local market and regulations

### International Considerations

- Time zone differences can slow communication
- Language barriers affect requirement gathering
- Legal jurisdiction matters for contracts
- Cultural differences in work practices

### Our recommendation

For projects under $500,000, local companies in Nepal offer the best balance of cost, communication, and quality. For larger or more complex projects, consider an international partner but ensure you have a clear contract, regular check-ins, and milestone-based payments.

## Common Pitfalls to Avoid

### 1. Choosing Based on Price Alone

The cheapest option often leads to the most expensive project. Poor code quality, missed requirements, and rework can cost 3–5× the initial savings.

### 2. Skipping References

Always call references. Ask:
- "Was the project delivered on time and on budget?"
- "How was communication throughout the project?"
- "Would you hire them again?"
- "What challenges did you face?"

### 3. Not Understanding the Contract

Read the fine print. Look for:
- Termination clauses
- Ownership transfer terms
- Liability limits
- Support obligations

### 4. Micromanaging

Hire a company you trust, then let them do their job. Constant oversight slows projects and frustrates developers. Set clear expectations upfront and trust the process.

### 5. Ignoring Post-Launch Needs

Your relationship with the development company should not end at launch. Ensure you have a plan for ongoing maintenance, updates, and improvements.

## Frequently Asked Questions

### How many vendors should I evaluate before making a decision?

At least 3. This gives you a baseline for pricing, quality, and process. More than 5 leads to analysis paralysis.

### What is a reasonable deposit to pay upfront?

10–30% of the total project cost. Never pay more than 50% upfront unless there are very specific deliverables tied to each payment.

### Should I sign an NDA before discussing my project?

Yes. A good development company will sign an NDA without hesitation. If they refuse, that is a red flag.

### What should be included in the contract?

Scope of work, timeline, payment schedule, ownership terms, support obligations, and termination clauses. If any of these are missing, push back.

### How long should a typical small business website take?

A basic website: 2–6 weeks. An ecommerce site: 6–12 weeks. A custom application: 3–6 months. If a company promises faster without compromising quality, ask how.

### What if I need ongoing development after launch?

Ensure the contract includes a maintenance and support plan. Most companies offer monthly retainers for ongoing work.

### Can I change requirements during development?

Yes, but scope changes should follow a formal change request process with associated cost adjustments. Avoid "just one small change" that cascades into major rework.

### How do I ensure the code quality is good?

Ask for code samples, review the documentation, and consider a third-party code review before final payment.

### What if the project goes over budget?

The contract should define what happens: additional cost estimates, revised timelines, or project termination options. Never accept unexpected overruns without a clear explanation.
